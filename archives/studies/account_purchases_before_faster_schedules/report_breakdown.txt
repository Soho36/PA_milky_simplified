The report asks: “Given a limited pot of money, when should we buy accounts, and how should we use payouts to keep the operation going?”
It replayed the same trading history with 108 combinations. The numbers are totals across the dataset, not annual income or a forecast.
What the purchase names mean
Name	In everyday language
monthly_one	Try to buy one account every month.
quarterly_one	Try to buy one account every three months.
quarterly_three	Try to buy three accounts together every three months.
replace_one	Keep one account running; replace it when it dies.
replace_five	Try to keep five accounts running.
reinvest_50pct / 100pct	Start with one account. Use half or all received payouts as the budget for additional accounts.
reinvest_restart_50pct / 100pct	Same, but if all accounts die, use available cash to buy a fresh account and try again.


Every account costs $200. Purchases happen only when there is enough cash, and no policy can have more than 20 live accounts.
“Reinvest 100%” means all payouts become available for purchases. It does not mean every dollar actually gets spent: the 20-account limit can leave plenty of cash unused.
How to read the money columns
Take the first winning row:
- Start with $1,000 and add nothing later.
- Buy 85 accounts over time, spending $17,000. Later purchases are financed by payouts.
- Finish with 20 accounts still alive.
- Receive $240,500 net during trading, after subtracting account purchases.
- The final permitted withdrawal adds about $189,972.
- Total net cash becomes $430,472.
- Add your original $1,000 back, and ending owner cash is $431,472.
The final withdrawal is one request allowed by the configured rules. It does not assume you can empty every account.
What the results are telling us
1. With only $1,000 and no top-ups, restarting and reinvesting works best among the tested choices.
Its best total is about $430k, versus $302k for buying monthly.
The restart rule matters enormously. Without it, the first account dies before paying anything. The policy then stops forever, losing $200 while $800 sits unused. That poor result tells us the policy needs a way to restart; it tells us little about whether 50% or 100% reinvestment is better.
2. With more funding available, buying three accounts quarterly comes out ahead.
Your funding	Best tested purchase approach	Total net cash
$1,000, no top-ups	Restart + reinvest 100%	$430k
$1,000 + $200/month	Three quarterly	$593k
$5,000, no top-ups	Three quarterly	$561k
$5,000 + $200/month	Three quarterly	$593k


Notice that the two $200/month scenarios produce the same profit. Starting with an extra $4,000 did not improve that winning policy’s result. It simply left $4,000 more of your own money in the ending cash balance.
3. Buying more accounts does not automatically make more money.
With $1,000 plus monthly funding:
- Three quarterly: 45 accounts bought → $593k
- One monthly: 60 accounts bought → $464k
- Restart and reinvest everything: 85 accounts bought → $430k
When the accounts start, how long they survive, and what they pay matter more than the purchase count alone.
4. “Most money during trading” and “most money after the final withdrawal” pick different settings.
The main tables select the best final total for each purchase policy.
For example, with $1,000 and no top-ups, restarting and reinvesting can produce:
- $269,100 during trading with daily withdrawal checks and a $31,900 retained balance.
- $430,472 total with monthly withdrawal checks and a $30,000 retained balance—but that version produces only $240,500 during trading.
So the highest final number can involve leaving substantially more money inside accounts until the end.
My main takeaway: the study shows that purchase timing and the ability to recover from early failures make a large difference. It also shows that extra funding eventually stops helping under the tested limits.
I would still treat “three quarterly is best” as a promising finding to check. Three accounts bought together experience the same stretch of trading, so this schedule may have landed on favourable dates in this dataset. Shifting the purchase months and changing the 20-account limit would tell us whether that advantage holds up.
