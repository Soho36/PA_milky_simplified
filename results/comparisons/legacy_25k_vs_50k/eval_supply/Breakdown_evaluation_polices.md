The table is more important than it first appears, because it answers a different question from the earlier withdrawal study.

Previously, the simulation effectively assumed:

> funded account dies → replacement funded account is immediately available.

Under that assumption, killing accounts can be economically rational. You extract money aggressively, sacrifice the account, replace it, and repeat. The account itself has little value because replacement has almost zero delay.

Once evaluations are introduced, that assumption disappears. An evaluation is a qualification stage before the next funded account becomes usable, so there is naturally a delay between losing earning capacity and restoring it.  Your own simulation makes that delay very substantial: median successful evaluation completion is about 35 days for 25K and 40 days for 50K.

Consider the 25K numbers:

| Scenario | Profit | Accounts consumed |
|---|---:|---:|
| Instant replacement, aggressive | **$810,670** | **674** |
| Evaluations, same aggressive policy | **$125–151K** | **204–213** |
| Evaluations, reoptimized | **$608,452** | **57** |

The first result says: *if I have an essentially unlimited warehouse of replacement accounts, burn them aggressively.*

The second says: *if every replacement must first be manufactured through an evaluation pipeline, the exact same policy breaks the factory.*

And the third result is especially interesting: **$608K from only 57 funded accounts.**

That's only about 25% less profit than the unrealistic $811K instant-supply result, while consuming roughly **91.5% fewer funded accounts**:

\[
1-\frac{57}{674}=91.5\%
\]

That is a very large efficiency improvement.

The 50K result tells almost exactly the same story. Instant supply produced $735K from 370 accounts; with realistic evaluation supply the unchanged policy collapses to roughly $177–192K. But after optimization, you get **$606K using only 44 accounts**. That's about 17.6% less profit than instant supply while using about **88% fewer accounts**.

So I would not interpret the study as saying "aggressive withdrawals are bad."

It's saying something subtler:

**Aggressive extraction is still good; aggressive account destruction is bad when replacements have lead time.**

That's why this finding is interesting:

> the reoptimized winners still withdraw maximum amounts daily, but retain $7,600/$8,000 above the frozen floor.

The optimizer hasn't suddenly become conservative about taking money out. It is still saying:

**"When surplus cash exists, take it."**

What changed is the definition of *surplus*. It now recognizes that part of the account balance has economic value as protection against having to wait 35–40 days for another funded account.

You can think about an active funded account almost like a productive machine.

Previously:

\[
Value(account) \approx future\ payouts
\]

because a broken machine could be replaced instantly.

Now:

\[
Value(account)
=
future\ payouts
+
replacement\ scarcity\ value
\]

That second term changes the optimum.

And this explains the huge collapse from $811K → ~$140K when you simply take the old policy and turn evaluations on. The strategy isn't suddenly less profitable. **Your production capacity becomes constrained.**

Imagine you have 20 taxis generating money. Your old optimization assumes that whenever one breaks, another Golf appears outside your house immediately. Under that assumption, running each car extremely hard can maximize income.

Then you change the model: a replacement Golf takes 35–40 days to arrive.

Suddenly the economically optimal maintenance policy changes dramatically, even though the revenue generated by driving hasn't changed.

That's essentially what your evaluation model has discovered.

There is another important clue in the account counts:

**674 → 204–213 → 57**

Why does the same aggressive policy consume only ~210 accounts once evaluations are enabled instead of 674?

Not because it suddenly became safer.

It simply **couldn't obtain enough replacements to kill 674 accounts.**

That's crucial.

The 204 number should therefore not be read as:

> "The evaluation version needed only 204 accounts."

It's closer to:

> "The evaluation bottleneck prevented the strategy from acquiring enough funded accounts to continue its previous turnover."

And that explains the catastrophic reduction in profit.

You had a highly profitable *account-burning engine* that required something like 674 funded accounts over 6½ years. Then you connected it to a factory incapable of producing them at that rate.

The engine spends much of its potential lifetime without enough fuel.

The reoptimization discovers another solution:

> Don't demand hundreds of replacement accounts. Make each funded account last much longer.

And suddenly the evaluation factory can keep up.

This is why I think your next study should **not yet compare another set of withdrawal amounts**. The major unanswered question is now:

**Where exactly is the replacement-capacity frontier?**

For example, perhaps 57 funded accounts isn't actually the economic optimum. Maybe the current evaluation implementation is artificially forcing the optimizer that low.

Suppose we progressively improve evaluation supply and obtain something like this hypothetical relationship:

| Pipeline capacity | Best funded turnover | Net extraction |
|---|---:|---:|
| current | 57 | $608K |
| +50% capacity | 90 | $650K |
| 2× capacity | 150 | $700K |
| 3× capacity | 250 | $750K |
| 5× capacity | 400 | $790K |
| unlimited | 674 | $811K |

I'm inventing those numbers only to illustrate what we need to measure.

If the real curve looks like that, then aggressive turnover is fundamentally good and the only problem is evaluation capacity.

But it could instead look like:

| Pipeline capacity | Best turnover | Net extraction |
|---|---:|---:|
| current | 57 | $608K |
| 2× | 65 | $620K |
| 5× | 72 | $625K |
| unlimited | 674 | $811K |

Then something different is happening: the final jump would mainly be an artifact of the unrealistic instant-replacement assumption.

There is also a third possibility, which I suspect is fairly plausible: **you don't need a permanently enormous evaluation pipeline; you need inventory.**

Your evaluations are correlated because evaluations launched together see identical strategy paths. Five simultaneous evaluations therefore aren't five independent replacement opportunities. A bad period can hurt the funded book and evaluation book simultaneously.

That suggests staggering could matter enormously.

Instead of:

`Funded die → start evaluations → wait 35–40 days → replacements`

you want something closer to:

`continuous evaluation pipeline → passed spare inventory → funded account dies → activate spare immediately`

Then the important variable becomes not merely evaluation pass rate but:

\[
\text{Replacement Service Level}
=
P(\text{spare available when funded account dies})
\]

That's a much better metric for this system than "97% pass within 180 days."

A 97% eventual pass rate sounds excellent. But if only 40% of 25K evaluations and 32% of 50K evaluations pass in the first month, that doesn't necessarily provide replacements when they're actually required.

In other words, **pass probability and supply reliability are different things.**

This also makes me think the "spare target 5/10" parameter is too crude. What matters is probably a combination of:

\[
\text{active evaluations}
+
\text{nearly-passed evaluations}
+
\text{passed inactive funded spares}
\]

against expected funded-account attrition.

Ultimately I'd like the simulator to tell us something like:

> Aggressive 25K withdrawal requires approximately X replacement funded accounts/month. To support that with 95% reliability, maintain Y concurrent staggered evaluations and Z funded spares. The required evaluation expenditure is $A/month, producing expected net extraction of $B.

**That would directly answer your original business question: "Can I replace them fast enough?"**

Right now we know the answer for the *current* implementation: **no**. We also know something more useful: once replacement scarcity is priced into the optimization, the system still recovers about $608K/$606K, which is surprisingly close to the $811K/$735K idealized upper case considering how dramatically turnover falls.

So I would view $608K/$606K not as evidence against aggressive withdrawals, but as evidence that **funded-account longevity and evaluation capacity need to be optimized jointly**.

And before trusting 57/44 as the final optimum, I would absolutely run the proposed pipeline-capacity experiment—persistent demand, staggered evaluations, different concurrency limits, and different spare inventories. That should tell us whether **57 accounts is genuinely optimal or merely what your current replacement factory can feed.**
