The main takeaway is: you can get much more of the money out earlier without reducing the final total—but you need to leave enough money in each account to survive losses.
Think of the retained balance as the account’s reserve. A $31,900 target means: “Only withdraw money above $31,900.” Trading losses can still push the balance below that level.
The four approaches tell this story:
- Take $500 monthly, keep $30,000: You receive about $266k along the way, then collect the rest at the end. Total: $461k.
- Check weekly for a $500 payout, keep $31,600: You receive about $350k along the way, with the same final total.
- Check daily for a $500 payout, keep $31,900: You receive about $358k along the way, again with the same final total.
- Check weekly and take everything allowed above $31,600: You receive about $369k along the way, but finish slightly lower, at $457k.
“Check daily” does not mean you receive $500 every day. It means asking whether the account has enough spare money and meets the firm’s payout rules.
The daily-$500 approach looks like a useful middle ground: it gets roughly $92k out earlier than monthly withdrawals, without giving up any final cash in this test.
The weekly “take all available excess” approach goes a little further: roughly $12k more cash earlier, in exchange for about $3k less overall.
But taking money aggressively every day was too much at the tested $31,600 reserve. It repeatedly left accounts vulnerable to later losses, and only two survived. So the lesson isn’t simply “withdraw more often.” It’s “withdraw more often in manageable amounts, while keeping a sufficient reserve.”
All four approaches in the table ended with 22 surviving accounts—the same number as holding everything. That means they extracted cash without cutting those accounts’ trading lives short on this dataset.
These are promising ways to operate based on this historical run, rather than promises about future earnings.

- Second table: “Which reserve produced the most cash during trading plus the final withdrawal?”

The first approach leaves a smaller reserve, allowing more withdrawals along the way. But it also leaves accounts more vulnerable to losses.
The second approach leaves an extra $1,900 in each account. It receives $15,400 less along the way, but preserves enough accounts to collect another $102,712 at the end.
The $0 final receipt does not mean the final withdrawal was switched off. It means the two surviving accounts could not make a permitted payout at that point. Being alive does not automatically mean an account qualifies for withdrawal.
Also, the final request is special: it tries to take the maximum the firm permits, releasing your voluntary reserve. It is not limited to another $500.
“Backlog” means carrying forward withdrawals you wanted but couldn’t take.
For example, fixed_750_backlog means:
“Add $750 to my withdrawal target each month. If I can’t collect it, carry it forward.”

Suppose three monthly targets accumulate without a payout:
- First month: $750 outstanding.
- Second month: $1,500 outstanding.
- Third month: $2,250 outstanding.
At the next successful check, the policy tries to collect that outstanding amount, subject to your reserve and the firm’s restrictions. If only $1,500 is permitted, $750 remains outstanding.
That backlog is your own bookkeeping target, not money the firm owes you or profit the account necessarily earned. Daily checks don’t add $750 daily; the target still increases only monthly.
By contrast, minimum_500_per_check has no backlog:
“Whenever a check succeeds, take $500. Failed checks don’t accumulate into a larger request.”

“Excess” means money above your chosen reserve.
With a $31,600 reserve and a $34,000 balance:
Excess = $34,000 − $31,600 = $2,400

maximum_excess_per_check tries to withdraw that $2,400, but the firm might cap it at a smaller amount or refuse it.
So the three policies are:
Policy	What it tries to take
Minimum $500	$500 whenever eligible
Fixed target with backlog	The accumulated, unpaid monthly target
Maximum excess	Everything available above your reserve


All three still respect the firm’s rules and your reserve during trading.
